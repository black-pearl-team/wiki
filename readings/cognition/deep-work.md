# DEEP WORK

## Concepts

> Deep work: hard, undistracted concentration on demanding work; it creates value and is hard to copy.

> Shallow work: easy, routine, often interrupted work; low value and easy to copy.

---

## Theory

### 1. Deep work is valuable

###### 1. Who comes out ahead in the new economy

   Smart machines are reshaping work, and **three groups gain the most [in the age of intelligent machines and the new economy]**

   - High-skilled workers: people who use intelligent machines well

     Automation removes routine jobs and raises the value of abstract, data-heavy work.

     Getting results out of complex machines pays.

   - Superstars: the best in their field

     A global talent market rewards the top and squeezes everyone else.

     Many of us now compete with the stars of our own field.

   - Owners: people with capital

     Those who fund new technology.

     With less need for labor, more of the return goes to capital.

How to get the first two:

- Learn hard tools fast
- Produce at an elite level, in quality and speed

> Easy consumer tech isn't a real tool; the powerful machines are complex.

> Skill alone isn't enough; you have to deliver results people value.

Both depend on the ability to work deeply.

###### 2. Deep work helps you learn hard things quickly

   - Deliberate practice

     Psychology: decades of research into what makes experts point to this idea.

     Neuroscience: focused, repeated use of one brain circuit builds myelin around it and makes the skill stick, which only works without distraction.

   **The core of deliberate practice:**

   a. full attention on the specific skill;
   b. feedback to correct your approach.

###### 3. Deep work makes elite-level output possible

   The best treat output as a problem to solve systematically.

   The formula: **high-quality output = time × intensity of focus**

   - *How to Become a Straight-A Student*

     The top students studied less than those just below them, because they focused harder.

   - Attention residue

     Switching tasks leaves part of your attention behind, and performance drops; the more residue, the worse.

     Long stretches on one task keep residue low.

   - What about executives?

     Their value is quick decisions across many inputs, so they hand the deep thinking to others.

### 2. Deep work is rare

1. **The metric black hole**

   Knowledge work is hard to measure.

   So the cost of distraction stays hidden.

2. **The principle of least resistance**

   Without clear feedback, we do what's easiest.

   > No measurement, easy choices.

   > Unexamined, this trades long-term value for short-term comfort.

3. **Busyness as a proxy for productivity**

   Visible activity stands in for real output. (Looking busy)

4. **Worship of the internet**

   We treat anything high-tech as good by default.

   The alternatives don't get banned; they just fade from view.

5. **Bad for business, good for you**

   The rarer deep work gets, the more it's worth.

### 3. Deep work is meaningful

1. **The neurological argument**
   What you attend to becomes your world.

   After hard times, where you put your focus shapes your outlook. (Taking charge of where your mind rests. Look on the bright side?)

   Deep effort builds a richer inner world.

   A day of shallow messages teaches your brain that work is only stress and trivia.

   An idle mind drifts toward worries.

2. **The psychological argument**

   Peak experiences come from stretching yourself on something hard and worthwhile. [Flow]

   Work, with its goals and feedback, can be more enjoyable than free time.

   Look for chances to get into flow at work.

3. **The philosophical argument**
   Craft is about seeing meaning, not making it up.

   Mastering any skill can feel sacred.

   Good code can feel like writing a poem.

   - *The Pragmatic Programmer*

     It opens with a medieval stonecutters' creed about keeping the cathedral in mind.

     Programmers, too, can bring craft and personality to their part of a bigger structure.

Treat your work as a craft and it can carry meaning, like a wheelwright's.

   The kind of job doesn't matter; the meaning lives in the skill.

   Do ordinary work in an uncommon way.

   Deep work can turn knowledge work into something satisfying (see *All Things Shining*).

## Rules

### 1. Work deeply

The main obstacle: the pull toward the shallow.

Willpower runs out, so rely on habits, not intentions.

The six strategies below are routines built around limited willpower.

Some steer your impulses; others recharge willpower.

1. **Choose your depth philosophy**

   - Monastic

     Cut shallow obligations almost entirely.

     Basically, becoming a hermit.

   - Bimodal

     Split your time into clear deep stretches and open stretches.

     The split can be by week or by season.

     People accept your absence if you announce it and are reachable the rest of the time.

   - Rhythmic

     1. The chain method

        Seinfeld's calendar trick: mark each working day and don't break the chain.

        A daily habit removes the question of when to go deep.

     2. Set a start time

        The same start time every day.

   - Journalistic

     Fit deep work in wherever there's room.

     It takes practice, and confidence that your work matters.

2. **Ritualize**

   > Mason Currey's research: waiting for inspiration is a bad plan.

   > Think like an artist, work like an accountant.

   Strict rituals make it easy to go deep and stay there.

  - Questions for a good ritual:

   1. Where, and for how long.

      A fixed place.

   2. How you'll keep going.

      Rules that give each session a structure.

   3. How you'll support it.

      Coffee, food, a walk, a tidy desk.

   Structure and commitment let focus happen.

3. **Make grand gestures**

   A big, unusual step (a retreat, a hotel room) signals that the work matters.

   The commitment, not the place, does the work.

4. **Don't work alone**

   Collaborate when it lifts the work, but protect your focus.

5. **Execute like a business**

   Carrying out a plan is harder than making one.

   Principles:

   1. Focus on what matters most

      Say yes to what you care about deeply, and it crowds out the rest.

   2. Act on lead measures

      (Track the process, not just the result.)

      Measure the behaviors you control that drive the goal.

      Here: hours of deep work.

   3. Keep a visible scoreboard

      Track the measure where you'll see it.

      1. It adds a competitive edge;
      2. It keeps you motivated.

      Deep hours should be the measure.

      1. It connects effort to results;
      2. It calibrates expectations.

   4. Review regularly

      - A weekly review sets up next week. (See Rule 4, point 2)

        Look back, learn from the bad weeks, plan for better ones.

6. **Be lazy**

   - 1. Downtime helps insight

       Unconscious thought can handle complex decisions well.

       It has more bandwidth than conscious thinking.

   1. Downtime recharges you
   2. Evening work is rarely that important

### 2. Embrace boredom

Focus is a skill you can train.

Adam Marlin's insight comes from daily training, not from deciding to think hard.

Reaching for distraction whenever you're bored undermines focus.

Train two things: concentration, and resisting distraction.

1. Don't keep getting distracted; keep focusing instead

   Schedule the occasional distraction instead of the occasional focus.

   Grabbing for novelty at every hint of boredom weakens focus.

2. Work like Roosevelt

   Set deadlines far tighter than your estimate.

   These sprints train focus.

3. Productive meditation

   Think through one problem while your body is busy (walking, driving, showering).

   1. Watch out for distraction and going in circles
   2. Give your thinking a structure

### 3. Quit social media

> The "any benefit" approach: use a tool if it offers any upside at all.

> The craftsman approach: use a tool only if it helps what matters most more than it hurts.

The craftsman approach weighs real benefits against real costs.

1. Apply the law of the vital few to your internet habits

   80% of results come from 20% of causes. [Do the important things first]

2. Quit social media

   It rewards attention-seeking over real value.

3. Don't use the internet to entertain yourself

   Clickbait eats time and attention. (Much of it is low quality.)

The mind needs change, not stopping.

Fill your waking hours with meaningful things and you'll feel better at the end of the day.

Live, don't just exist.

### 4. Drain the shallows

1. Plan every minute of your day

   Ask what's most sensible to do with the time that's left.

   Respect your own time. 

2. Quantify the depth of every activity

   Ask how long a smart graduate would need to learn to do it.

   The longer, the deeper.

3. Ask your boss for a shallow work budget

   Say no to purely shallow projects, and cut shallow work wherever you can.

   A deep-to-shallow ratio keeps shallow work contained.

4. Finish your work by 5:30

   Make time for what keeps you happy.

   Guard your personal time.

5. Become hard to reach

   Let small bad things happen to make room for the big ones.

   (Small bad things, like someone getting annoyed because you didn't reply right away)
